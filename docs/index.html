A swapped candidate caught in 38 seconds, not 77

Online interviews and exams usually check a face once, at login. Swapdetect keeps checking during the session. On simulated webcam problems with 91 actors, it flagged a swap about 50% faster than a tuned version of a 2026 published method, at similar false-alarm rates.

The problem

A person can log in as the candidate and then hand the seat to someone else. A login-only check never notices. Checking the face every few seconds helps, but real webcams fail often: poor light, a turned head, a missing face. A system that treats every failed check as suspicious raises too many false alarms, and one that ignores failures reacts slowly.

The reference paper

We compare against Continuous Face Authentication on Mobile and Desktop Platforms: A Comparative Study by Palmetshofer, Findling and Kurz (arXiv 2608.00763). It keeps a trust score that decays over time and drops to zero when no face is seen, then locks when trust falls below a threshold. We reimplemented its equations on the same features and the same people as our method, so any difference comes from the method, not a stronger face model. This is our reimplementation, not the authors' own system or data.

What we changed

Decision ruleA change detector (CUSUM) calibrated to a false-alarm target replaces a fixed trust threshold.
EvidenceFace and voice are fused with learned weights, instead of a face-only score.
Failure handlingA "face found" signal lets the system treat a missing face as missing evidence, not as an impostor.

What we claim

Against a reimplementation of the temporal-decay authenticator of Palmetshofer et al. (2026), tuned on calibration people, our method detected swaps about 50% faster (mean 50.5%, range 49.4 to 52.5 across 3 seeds), at comparable false-alarm rates (0.169 vs 0.145 per hour), on simulated webcam degradation with 91 acted studio people.

What we do not claim

  • The quality score is not shown to help. Adding it changed delay by +2.2 s, +2.5 s and −2.0 s across the three seeds, so the sign flips. Most of the gain comes from voice fusion and the failure flag, which we tested together.
  • False alarms are close but not equal. Ours average 0.169 per hour and the tuned baseline 0.145.
  • The baseline's decay setting often hit the top of our search range (6000 s, which is almost no decay). We treat that as its strongest form.
  • Not tested: real exams, real webcams, photo or replay attacks, and attackers who try to avoid detection. The data is acted studio clips with simulated degradation.
  • The paper's own published settings give about 17 false alarms per hour on our data, so its threshold has to be recalibrated for new data.
